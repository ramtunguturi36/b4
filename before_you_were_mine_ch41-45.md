# **Before You Were Mine**
### *Chapters 41–45*

## CHAPTER 41 — The Last Night Before Break

Saturday arrived the way last days always did — too quickly and with a specific quality of light that seemed to know it was the last of something.

Kiran's train was at 6 AM Sunday. Arjun's flight was at 9. They had Saturday evening, which was not enough and also exactly enough because that was the nature of time when you'd finally arrived somewhere and immediately had to leave.

Suresh had already packed and gone — a Friday evening bus, home to Warangal, a cheerful and completely oblivious goodbye that consisted of fistbumps and a reminder about borrowing Kiran's calculator over the summer which Kiran granted without paying attention to.

The room was just Kiran's.

Arjun appeared at seven with his own bag — packed for the flight, which meant he'd spent the last night in his own room and come here first. He stood in the doorway with the bag over his shoulder and looked at the room — at the space of it, Suresh's side cleared, the particular quiet of an emptying hostel at summer start.

"I don't want to go to Delhi," he said.

"I know," Kiran said.

"My mother's agenda—"

"I know."

"It's laminated, Kiran."

"You mentioned."

Arjun came in. He dropped his bag. He sat in his spot on the bed — the last time, this summer, on this bed in this room — and looked at Kiran with the expression that was still new enough that Kiran noticed it specifically: open, direct, not managing anything.

They ordered food — not canteen, actual food, from the place near the gate that did decent biryani and stayed open late on semester-end Saturdays because they understood their customer base. They ate sitting on the floor of the room because the desk was covered in Kiran's packed things and the bed had Arjun's bag on it.

They ate and talked about the summer — Arjun's Delhi schedule, Kiran's Vizag routine, the mutual agreement that two weeks was survivable but annoying. They talked about third year, which was beginning to feel real now. Different courses. Different pressures. Still the same hostel block.

After dinner Arjun lay back on the floor, arms behind his head, looking at the ceiling.

Kiran looked at him.

The yellow of the room light. His face relaxed. The rise and fall of his chest.

"Come here," Arjun said.

"I'm here."

"Come here," he said again. Looking at the ceiling. An open hand.

Kiran lay down beside him.

Side by side on the floor of his hostel room, looking at the ceiling — the same ceiling Kiran had looked at for two years, the same water stain in the corner that looked like a running dog, the same slight unevenness near the fan.

Arjun's hand found his between them.

"Tell me something you haven't told anyone," Arjun said. The question he always asked. Kiran's question now, said back to him.

Kiran thought about it.

"In October," he said, "after the fest. I walked back the long way, through the path behind the canteen. And I said your name out loud to myself. Just — to hear what it was." He paused. "I'd known for about an hour. I needed to hear it externally."

Arjun was quiet.

"What did it sound like," he said.

"Like something I'd already decided," Kiran said.

Arjun turned his head. Looking at Kiran on the floor. Kiran looked back.

"I was in the library once," Arjun said. "Early March. You'd gone to get water. You'd left your notebook open." A pause. "I read the last line on the page. I don't know why. I never read people's notes."

Kiran looked at him. "What did it say?"

Arjun held his gaze.

"It said: *the quality of his attention is the most dangerous thing about him.*" He said it slowly, like he'd been keeping it. "I didn't know what to do with that for two weeks."

Kiran felt that land.

"I didn't know you'd read that," he said.

"I know you didn't." His thumb moved across Kiran's knuckles. "I should have said something then. I think I already knew then. I just didn't have—"

"The language," Kiran said.

"Yes." He exhaled. "I didn't have the language."

Kiran looked at the ceiling.

"You have it now," he said.

"I'm learning it." He squeezed Kiran's hand once, briefly. "I'm going to keep learning it."

They lay on the floor until midnight.

At midnight Kiran said, "You should sleep. Your flight—"

"I know." Arjun didn't move for a moment. Then he turned onto his side, facing Kiran. This close. The floor. The late-night quiet of the emptying hostel.

He put his hand against the side of Kiran's face.

The same palm. The same warmth. The gesture that had first appeared at a desk months ago — tentative then, certain now.

"Two weeks," he said.

"Two weeks," Kiran confirmed.

"I'll message from the airport."

"I know you will."

"I'll message from Delhi."

"I know."

"I'll message from—"

"Arjun." Kiran covered his hand with his own. "I know."

Arjun looked at him. The expression at its fullest — nothing held back, nothing managed.

He leaned forward and kissed Kiran — slow, soft, the goodnight version, the *I'll be back* version. His hand warm against the side of Kiran's face. Kiran's hand over his. The floor beneath them and the city outside and the summer beginning.

He pulled back.

"Two weeks," he said again.

"Two weeks," Kiran said.

Arjun stood. Picked up his bag. Looked at the room one more time — the ceiling, the water stain, Kiran on the floor looking up at him.

"Don't let anyone sit in your library seat," he said.

Kiran smiled. "It's your seat too."

Something crossed Arjun's face — something complete and warm and entirely undone.

"Yeah," he said quietly. "It is."

He left.

Kiran lay on the floor of his empty room and looked at the water stain on the ceiling.

He was smiling so widely that it almost hurt.

He stayed there until 1 AM.

Then he got up, finished packing, set his alarm, and went to sleep.

## CHAPTER 42 — Two Weeks (The Messages Change)

The first message came before Kiran's train reached Vizag.

*at the airport. security is a nightmare. they made me take off my shoes and then looked disappointed when I wasn't hiding anything interesting*

Kiran was in his train seat, the landscape of Andhra Pradesh moving past his window, and he read the message and felt the particular warmth of knowing exactly who was on the other end of it — the specific Arjun of it, the tone, the voice.

*You should have hidden something interesting,* he typed. *Better story.*

*noted for next time. how's the train*

*Good. Tea is not a crime against humanity this time.*

*progress. the bar is low but progress.*

This was how the messages changed: not in frequency — they'd been messaging daily since the first holiday break — but in texture. Something had unlocked. Before, Kiran had been careful with his messages. He'd chosen words with an awareness of what they might reveal, what they might ask for, what they might suggest. The management had been present even in small exchanges.

Now there was no management.

He said things he would have previously thought twice about. He said *I miss the roof* on day three and did not immediately analyze whether that was too much. He said *I was thinking about you when I was on the beach this morning* on day five and then put his phone in his pocket and walked into the sea up to his knees and felt the cold water and thought: *this is what it feels like to want something and just say so.*

Arjun's messages changed too.

They became longer. More specific. Less performed. He described Delhi in the way he described things when he wasn't thinking about how he was describing them — the chaos of his mother's agenda, the dinner party he'd been required to attend, his grandmother who had taken one look at him and said *you look happy, something happened* with the accuracy of grandmothers everywhere.

*What did you tell her,* Kiran asked.

*I said I made a friend.*

A pause. Then:

*she said 'what kind of friend' and I said 'the important kind' and she said 'good, bring them next time' and went back to her chai*

Kiran read this three times.

*The important kind,* he typed.

*the most important kind,* Arjun replied. *obviously.*

On day seven Arjun sent a voice note.

This was new. They had texted for months — voice notes were for people who were comfortable enough to hear each other's voices in ordinary moments, not just calls. Kiran put his earphones in, sitting on the Vizag beach at the hour he always came, the early morning light coming off the water.

Arjun's voice, unhurried: *I was walking back from dinner and I passed this bookshop — proper old one, the kind you like, paper smell and everything — and I thought you'd want to know it exists. I took a photo. Sending it after this. Also my mother asked about my third year plans and I accidentally said something about a person and she did the thing where she doesn't ask but asks with her eyes and I had to leave the room. Anyway. Hi. It's midnight here. I hope the beach was good today.*

The photo arrived after. A bookshop, narrow, warm-lit, the kind with books stacked horizontally because there was no more vertical space. Old wooden shelves. A cat visible on the windowsill.

Kiran looked at it for a long time.

*It's perfect,* he typed. *Save me a book.*

*already bought you one. don't ask what it is it's a surprise.*

*You bought me a book.*

*is that okay*

Kiran looked at the sea.

*More than okay,* he typed.

He put his phone in his pocket and watched the water and let the full quiet happiness of it sit in him without trying to do anything with it.

*The most important kind.* He'd bought him a book from a Delhi bookshop because he'd seen it and thought of him.

He thought: *I have never been someone's person like this before.*

He thought: *I think I could get used to it.*

On day ten Arjun called.

Not a voice note. A call — Sunday evening, unannounced. Kiran was on the rooftop of his house in Vizag — his childhood roof, different from the hostel roof but serving a similar function. He answered.

"Hey," Arjun said.

"Hey," Kiran said.

Silence. But not empty — the comfortable kind, the kind that worked over phone the way it worked in person, which was unusual and specific to them.

"Four more days," Arjun said.

"Four more days," Kiran said.

"I'm ready to be back."

"I know."

"Are you—" A pause. "Are you ready."

Kiran looked at the Vizag skyline going orange at the edges.

"Yes," he said. "I'm ready."

He heard Arjun exhale.

"Good," Arjun said. Quiet. "Good."

They talked for an hour about nothing important and everything real and Kiran sat on his childhood roof with the sea somewhere in the distance and thought about another roof entirely and felt, for the first time in a long time, that the distance between where he was and where he was going was not a loss but a direction.

Four more days.

## CHAPTER 43 — Coming Back

The train pulled into Hyderabad at 7 AM.

Kiran sat in his seat as the city arrived around the windows — the particular approach of Hyderabad, the density of it, the way it assembled itself from the outside in as the train slowed. He had his bag in the overhead rack and his book in his lap and he had not read a single page in the last hour.

His phone buzzed at 7:03.

*landed. just cleared baggage. where are you*

*Train just arrived,* he typed.

*I'll come to the station*

Kiran looked at the message.

*You don't have to—*

*kiran.*

He smiled.

*Platform 3,* he typed.

He got off the train into the familiar noise of the Hyderabad station — the heat already building even at this hour, the press of people, the announcements overlapping in Hindi and Telugu. He had his bag over his shoulder and he was looking for a familiar face before he'd decided to look.

Arjun found him first.

He appeared from the direction of the main concourse — bag over his shoulder, slightly airport-disheveled, moving through the crowd with his usual ease that was less about physical grace and more about a certain kind of confidence in occupying space. He saw Kiran and his face did the thing.

The thing.

The thing Kiran had been cataloguing for a year. Except now he knew what it was and what it meant and he could let himself see it fully.

"Hey," Arjun said.

He was standing in front of Kiran in the noise and crowd and heat of Hyderabad station and he was looking at him the way he looked at him, and Kiran thought:

*This is mine. This person is mine.*

Not possessively. Just: truly. The way a place is yours, or a language is yours — something that has become part of the structure of you.

"Hey," Kiran said.

Arjun reached out and took the bag from Kiran's shoulder — just lifted the strap off without asking, transferred it to his own — and then his free hand found the back of Kiran's neck. Warm. Brief. The station moving around them.

"Hi," he said again. Softer. Just for Kiran.

"Hi," Kiran said. Just as soft.

They stood there for a moment — two people in a busy station, bags and two weeks of distance and one summer between them — and then Arjun's forehead found Kiran's in the way it had learned to. Just a touch. Just: *here. back. this.*

Kiran closed his eyes.

"Good summer?" Arjun murmured.

"Better now," Kiran said.

He felt Arjun smile.

They took an auto back. The city moved around them — familiar, loud, unchanged. Their arms pressed together in the backseat the way they always did, and Arjun's hand found Kiran's on the seat between them, and the auto moved through Hyderabad traffic with its usual disregard for personal safety, and everything was — returned.

Not the same as before. Better than before.

*Back. But better.*

At the hostel gate Arjun produced a book from his bag — the Delhi bookshop book, wrapped in newspaper in the way of shops that didn't have gift wrap but understood presentation. He held it out.

Kiran took it.

He turned it over. The cover — a travel memoir, old, a writer he'd mentioned once in passing in a conversation Kiran barely remembered having.

"You remembered that," Kiran said.

"I remember most things you tell me," Arjun said. Echoing Kiran's words from weeks ago. A small deliberate callback.

Kiran looked at the book.

He looked at Arjun.

He said nothing.

He didn't need to.

Arjun seemed to understand this. He reached over, tucked a strand of Kiran's hair back — two fingers, the gesture — and then turned toward the hostel.

"Third year," he said.

"Third year," Kiran said.

They went in.

## CHAPTER 44 — Third Year Begins — Everything And Nothing Has Changed

The hostel was loud with return.

Everyone arriving simultaneously — bags and summer stories and complaints about family and the particular chaos of a hundred people reclaiming their rooms and their routines and their social territories after two months of dispersal. The corridors were full. The canteen was full. The energy was the specific energy of beginning — not quite fresh, more like a reset. A new year but in a place you already knew.

Kiran found his room — different room, third year allocation, he'd known about this but the actuality of it was slightly strange. Third floor now instead of second. A window facing north. Suresh was his roommate again, which was continuity he appreciated.

He unpacked. The room slowly became his — textbooks on the shelf, the specific arrangement he always used, the lamp from home his mother had insisted on. He stood in the centre of it and looked around and thought: *new room. Same everything else.*

He looked at the drawer.

He'd transferred the photograph and the note to this room before the break. They were in the new drawer now — the same objects, different location. He thought about this briefly and then stopped thinking about it because it was the kind of thought that didn't need more time.

Arjun's room was two floors down and one wing over. Still close enough. Possibly more distance than before, measured in corridor length, but that measurement had become irrelevant some time around May.

The first week of third year established itself as different in a low-grade continuous way. Harder subjects. More project work. A sense of direction emerging that hadn't been present before — what came after, what was being prepared for, the future making itself felt in the present.

They still walked to the canteen together. They still had the library. The roof had been reclaimed — first evening back, without discussion, both of them ending up there at nine, sitting in their spots on the parapet wall as if the summer had been a pause in a sentence rather than an interruption.

But other things had changed:

In the canteen now, Arjun sat with his shoulder pressed against Kiran's without the old management of it — not incidental contact but chosen contact, the way you sat next to someone you'd decided about. It was small. It was visible to anyone paying attention.

In the library, their books were sometimes side by side rather than parallel — Arjun leaning over to look at something in Kiran's text, not always because he needed to, sometimes just because the proximity was available and he'd decided to take it.

On the walk back from lecture, Arjun's hand would find the back of Kiran's neck for the length of a few steps — brief, easy, the kind of touch that existed in the register of *I know you're here* rather than *look at what we are.*

The world had not changed around them. The campus continued. The lectures continued. The collective social machinery of 500 engineering students going about their lives continued. But there was a warmth in the specific geography of Kiran's days that had not been there before, a brightness in the ordinary that came from the simple fact of being known.

Of being chosen.

Of someone walking into a canteen and finding him first in the room.

Priya, on day three, said: "You're different."

"In what way."

"Lighter." She considered. "You've always been composed. Now you're composed and light. It's a good combination."

Kiran thought about this.

"I'm not managing as much," he said.

"I can tell." She smiled. "It suits you."

He thought about that as he walked back. *Not managing as much.* He thought about all the architecture he'd built over nine months — the rules, the distances, the careful rationing. Some of it had been necessary. Some of it had been excessive, the overengineering of someone who'd learned early that wanting visible things was dangerous.

He was learning to put some of it down.

It was slower than he'd expected. Old habits had mass. But he was learning.

That evening on the roof Arjun said, out of nowhere, into the comfortable dark: "I told Suresh."

Kiran looked at him.

"What did he say."

"He said, and I'm quoting directly: *bhai finally.*"

Kiran looked at the city.

"That's it?"

"That's it." Arjun's shoulder pressed into his. "He seemed — unsurprised. Possibly relieved."

"Suresh has known since approximately February," Kiran said.

"February—" Arjun turned. "How long has everyone known."

"Priya since October. Suresh since February. Dilip since at least November." He paused. "Dilip apparently called it in November."

Arjun stared at him.

"Dilip," he said.

"Dilip."

A pause.

"I was," Arjun said carefully, "apparently the last to know."

"About your own feelings," Kiran said, "yes."

Arjun looked at the city for a long moment. Then he dropped his head back and laughed — the full unguarded version, the real one — and Kiran watched him laugh at the sky with the particular fondness of someone who had been given the gift of knowing a person completely.

"Okay," Arjun said, still laughing slightly. "Fair."

"Very fair."

He turned, still smiling, and found Kiran already looking at him.

"Stop looking at me like that," he said.

"Like what."

"Like that." He gestured vaguely at Kiran's face. "The fond thing."

"I've been looking at you like this for a year," Kiran said. "You just didn't know what it was."

Arjun went quiet for a moment.

Then: "I know what it is now."

"Yes," Kiran said.

"I like it," Arjun said simply.

"Good," Kiran said. "Because it's not going anywhere."

Arjun's hand found his on the parapet.

The city below. The September start of third year. Everything the same and everything different in the specific way of things that have been named.

Neither of them let go.

## CHAPTER 45 — The First Time Someone Asks Directly

It was Tanvi who asked.

Not Priya — Priya already knew. Not Suresh — Suresh had adopted a policy of knowing without requiring confirmation, which Kiran respected. Not Dilip — Dilip processed information slowly and would probably arrive at asking sometime in fourth year.

Tanvi. Who had been joining their lunch group occasionally since before the break and who was, Kiran had observed, more direct than most people gave her credit for.

It was a Wednesday in September. The canteen at lunch, the usual table, Arjun mid-story about something that had happened in his morning project group. Kiran was eating and listening and at some point Arjun had made a point — something about his professor's instructions — and looked at Kiran specifically, the way he did when he wanted Kiran's opinion, and Kiran had said *you're right but—* and Arjun had said *I know, I know, but—* and they'd gone back and forth briefly in the shorthand of people who knew how each other thought.

Tanvi had watched this exchange.

Then she looked at Priya.

Then she looked back at Kiran.

"Are you two together?" she said.

Direct. Not unkind. Just: asking the thing.

The table went the specific quiet of people who knew the answer and were waiting to see how the people being asked would handle it.

Kiran put his fork down.

He looked at Arjun.

Arjun looked at him.

Something passed between them — brief, complete. A conversation conducted in the language they'd been building for a year, which now required no words for most of its content.

*Your call,* Kiran's expression said.

Arjun looked at Tanvi.

"Yes," he said. Easily. Completely. No qualification.

Tanvi nodded. "Okay," she said. Like she was filing information. "I thought so." She went back to her food.

The table held a moment of collective adjustment — not negative, just the small recalibration of people updating their understanding of a situation. Suresh was very occupied with his rice. Dilip appeared to be processing. Priya had achieved the expression of someone who had just been proved right about something and was virtuously not saying so.

"Since when?" Dilip said.

"May," Arjun said.

"Before the break?"

"Yes."

Dilip looked at the ceiling briefly. "I thought something was different at the end of semester." He looked at Kiran. "You seemed — I don't know. Like something had resolved."

"It had," Kiran said.

"Good," Dilip said simply. He ate a forkful of rice. "Makes sense."

Which was — that was it. That was the conversation. The matter-of-fact normality of it, the complete absence of drama, the table simply updating and moving on.

Kiran looked at his food.

He thought about October, walking the long path behind the canteen saying a name to himself. He thought about a year of careful management and rationing and rules and the weight of loving something quietly. He thought about the corridor outside room 207 and four words through a door and the specific cost of *just.*

He thought about: *yes. easily. completely. no qualification.*

He looked at Arjun.

Arjun was already looking at him. The expression — the real one, the particular one. Something warm in it. Something that recognized what Kiran was thinking about and had its own response ready.

*I know,* it said. *I know it took time. I know what it cost. I'm here now.*

Kiran looked at his food.

He picked up his fork.

Under the table, Arjun's knee found his.

Stayed.

"—anyway," Arjun said, resuming his story about the project group, his voice completely even, the most natural thing in the world, "what my professor actually said was—"

The table resumed. The canteen continued. The Wednesday continued.

Kiran ate his lunch.

He thought: *I am sitting at a table in a college canteen in Hyderabad and the person beside me just said yes without hesitating and I am — I am okay. I am more than okay.*

He thought: *this is what it feels like to not be managing.*

He took a breath.

He was right.

This was exactly what it felt like.
